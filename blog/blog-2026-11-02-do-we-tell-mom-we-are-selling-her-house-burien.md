# Do We Tell Mom We're Selling Her House? What to Do When She Isn't Ready to Let Go

Here is a post I keep thinking about. A woman moved her mother into assisted living in the spring. Her mother was not ready to give up her house, so it has not been listed. It sits there, and her mother sometimes talks about going back. The daughter noticed something: the idea of the house seemed to be holding her mother together. Then a neighbor's friend offered to buy it as is, furnished, and the minute the sale felt real, her mother came apart. She did not want assisted living to be permanent.

The daughter can afford to keep the house, so money is not forcing her hand. Her brother wants to take the offer. She is the one who sits with their mother every day and watches her face. She wrote that she feels stuck, and then she wrote the line that stopped me: she wished her mother were further along in the disease so that she would not feel any of this.

I am paraphrasing, because it is her story and not mine. If you are reading this because there is a house somewhere in your family right now, with the lights off and the mail piling up and a parent who keeps asking about it, I want to say the first thing out loud: this is one of the hardest parts of a care transition, and almost nobody warns you about it. You are not being dramatic. A house is never just a house.

It gets much less tangled once you can see its pieces. Let's pull them apart.

## Should we tell Mom we're selling her house?

Usually yes, but as a short, calm conversation and not a verdict, and only after you have settled the two other questions hiding inside this one. Telling her is the third decision in a set of three, and families who try to solve all three at once are the ones who get stuck.

The three decisions are these. **The money decision:** what the house costs to keep, what it could sell for, and how that fits with paying for care. It has numbers and deadlines, and it belongs to the adults. **The timing decision:** when, if ever, it gets listed. The answer can be next month or next spring, and "after the holidays" is a legitimate answer. **The telling decision:** what your mother hears, when, and from whom. It belongs to her heart, and it should be built around how she is doing today, not around how fast an agent wants to move.

When these are tangled, every conversation about one becomes a fight about all three. The sibling who says "sell it" is usually talking about the money. The sibling who says "she's not ready" is usually talking about the telling. Both are right, and they are having two different arguments at once.

If your mother can follow and take part, she should hear it from you, early, before anyone else mentions it. Surprises turn a hard truth into a betrayal. If she lives with dementia, the telling usually needs to be smaller and closer to the event, and you may decide that repeating the news would only hurt her fresh each time she forgot it. I wrote about starting this kind of conversation in [what to do when a parent won't talk about care](/blog/parent-wont-talk-about-care-burien). You are not trying to win agreement. You are trying to protect her sense that she is still treated as a person.

## Why is the house so hard for her to let go of?

For many people the house is the last place where they are still the one who decides. Everything else has been handed over: the schedule, the meals, the medications, the appointments. The house, with her name on the deed, is proof that she is still in charge of something.

There is also a quieter reason. In the first frightening weeks, many families soften the move with a promise. "It's just a trial." "We'll see how it goes." Nobody says it to lie. They say it because the alternative feels cruel. But a trial period is a debt, and the house is where she keeps the receipt. When the sign goes up, she hears the promise breaking.

What she is grieving is rarely the walls. It is the kitchen where she knew where everything was, the chair where she read the paper, the quilt, the sound of the porch step. It is a whole self made of small objects, and the house is the container. If you can move the objects, you can move a good deal of the self. I will come back to that. And if this sounds like the grief of watching a parent change in front of you, it is the same grief, and [this piece on ambiguous loss](/blog/ambiguous-loss-dementia-parent-burien) may be worth reading tonight.

## What does an empty house actually cost?

More than most families expect, every month, which is why it works like a clock. The clock is not a reason to panic. It is a reason to know your number.

Add up property tax, homeowner's insurance, utilities (heat in a Burien winter is not optional if you want to avoid frozen pipes), yard and gutter care, storm cleanup, and someone who checks on the place weekly. Many homeowner policies limit coverage when a house sits vacant for a month or two, so call your insurance agent this week and ask what yours says. Do it before a leak, not after.

Then there is the cost that never shows up on a statement. Someone has to drive over, sort the mail, answer the neighbor, and stand in the hallway deciding what to do with a late husband's coat. That someone is usually the same person already visiting the care home three times a week. A vacant house quietly adds a second job to the person doing the first.

None of this means you must sell right away. A house can be a good bridge for a season, and some families find that knowing it is still there keeps a parent steadier through the hardest first weeks. Some rent it out, and the income helps with care. If you go that way, treat it as a plan with an end date and a number, not a way to avoid deciding, and talk to your accountant before you become a landlord.

## Does Medicaid make us sell the house in Washington?

Not while your parent is living, and the rules are more forgiving than most families fear, but the details matter and they change. In Washington, a person's home is generally treated as an exempt resource when they apply for COPES, the Medicaid program that helps pay for adult family home care. The state's plain-language legal aid materials say it directly: some resources do not count, including your home. I walk through the whole process in [the COPES and Medicaid guide](/blog/copes-medicaid-adult-family-home-burien).

Three things are worth knowing before you decide anything about the house.

**The five-year look-back.** If a parent gives away assets in the five years before applying for COPES, the state can impose a penalty period during which it will not pay. The penalty is the value of the gift divided by a daily nursing facility rate that the state updates each year. Signing the house over to a child "to protect it" is one of the most common mistakes families make, and it can cost months of coverage.

**Estate recovery.** After a Medicaid recipient dies, DSHS may seek repayment from the estate, which can include a lien on a home the person owned. This is a big reason to see an attorney before you transfer or sell anything.

**Narrow exceptions.** Some transfers are protected, including to a child who lived in the home and provided care for at least two years before the parent needed COPES, with proof. The paperwork is where families get tripped up.

So here is the rule I would write on every family's refrigerator: do not sign anything that transfers the house until you have talked to a Washington elder law attorney. Not a friend who went through this, and not a website, including this one. One hour with the right attorney can protect tens of thousands of dollars. For a free first call, Community Living Connections (855-567-0252) can point you to Washington's long-term care programs and help you find who to talk to next.

## Who has the legal right to sell Mom's house?

Whoever owns it, or whoever she has legally authorized to act for her. If your mother signed a durable power of attorney while she could understand it, and it covers real estate, her agent may be able to sell for her. If she did not, and she can no longer understand and sign, the family may be looking at guardianship, which is slower, more public, and more expensive.

Read the power of attorney itself, because it has to say what it covers and a title company will read every word. Remember that the agent owes your mother a duty to act in her best interest, not in the interest of the sibling who is tired or the sibling who inherits. That duty can argue for selling, and it can argue against rushing a sale at a poor price. If more than one person owns the home, or it sits in a trust, the rules change again. And if there is no power of attorney and your parent can still sign, make that appointment this month. Waiting for a crisis makes it harder, and sometimes impossible.

## What are the tax questions to ask before listing it?

The main ones are the home sale exclusion, the cost basis, and the closing costs, and each deserves a call to a CPA before you accept an offer. I am not a tax professional, so please treat these as questions, not answers.

One forum reply on the post I mentioned noticed something many families miss: the federal home sale exclusion generally requires that the seller lived in the home for two of the last five years. There is a special rule for people who move into a licensed care facility, and it can let time in the facility count toward that test. Whether it applies depends on dates only a professional can check.

Ask also about "stepped-up basis," the tax rule for what a house is worth when it is inherited versus sold during a lifetime. The results can differ a great deal, and in a Medicaid situation the estate recovery rules pull the other way. That is why the CPA and the elder law attorney should talk to each other. In Washington the seller also pays real estate excise tax at closing, so ask your agent for the real number early.

## How do we tell her without breaking her heart?

In small, true sentences, without promises you cannot keep, and with something for her to hold on to. Here is what I have seen work.

**Keep the news short and calm.** "Mom, we've decided the house needs a new owner so you don't have to worry about it anymore. I've got it handled." Then stop. The more you explain, the more doors you open for an argument.

**Be careful with a fib that someone has to maintain.** Some caregivers suggest telling a parent with dementia that the deal fell through, and for some families that is the kindest path. But every family member has to keep that story, indefinitely, including the brother who lives far away. If you choose a softer version of the truth, write the one sentence down and send it to every sibling.

**Do not promise she is going home.** When she keeps asking, answer the feeling, not the address: "I know you miss it. Tell me about the kitchen." Underneath "I want to go home" is almost always "I want to feel safe and like myself."

**Think hard before a last visit.** Many families feel the pull, and the replies on one caregiver thread I read were mostly against it, because a final walk-through can stir up grief and confusion without delivering a goodbye. If your mother is clear-minded and asks, the answer may be different. Follow her, not the plan.

**Build a home box.** Before the house is cleared, choose the things that carry her: the quilt, the cardigan, the reading chair, the ticking clock, the cookbook with her handwriting in the margins. Put them in her room, and photograph every room of the house so it exists somewhere she can visit. A room that looks like hers does more than any speech you can give.

**Do the hard day without her.** The cleanout, the showings, and the closing are for the adults. She does not need to watch a stranger open her cabinets. Have someone with her that day, plan a favorite snack, and give yourself two minutes in the car afterward. You will need them.

## How do we keep the siblings from splitting over it?

By agreeing on facts first and feelings second, on one page, with a date. In the post I mentioned, the brother wanted to sell and the sister wanted to wait, and neither said out loud that they were protecting different things. He was protecting the family's money and their mother's security. She was protecting their mother's peace, and herself from having to witness the distress. Both are legitimate.

Write down the monthly cost of holding the house, what similar homes are selling for, what care costs, and a date, perhaps sixty or ninety days out, to review the plan. The sibling who sees your mother daily gets a real vote, and so does the sibling who handles the money. No one gets to be the villain. If you are the one doing most of the work, [this piece on being the only one](/blog/sole-caregiver-siblings-burien) was written for you.

## What if I'm not ready to sell either?

Then you are allowed to not be ready, and the house can wait. Holding it through the first holiday season to see how your parent settles, then deciding with a clearer head in the spring, is not failure. What matters is that you are choosing, with numbers on the table, instead of drifting.

When you are ready, selling is its own kind of work. ## A last thought, for the person at the kitchen table

The woman who wrote that post wished her mother were further along so she would not feel it. I understand that wish, and I want to say something to her and to you. The distress is not proof you are doing it wrong. It is a sign that your mother is still here, still herself, still attached to the life she built. The very thing that makes this so painful is the thing worth protecting.

You cannot make the grief disappear. But you can sequence the decisions so the money does not rush the heart, carry her things into her new room so she is not left with nothing, and say one gentle sentence at a time. That is a lot. It is also enough.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I have to tell my parent with dementia that her house is being sold?

There is no single rule. If she can understand and take part, tell her early, in a few calm sentences. If she lives with dementia, many families choose a shorter, simpler message close to the event, and agree as siblings on one sentence everyone uses. An elder law attorney can advise on the legal side.

### Does Washington Medicaid take my parent's house?

Not while she is living. Washington generally treats the home as an exempt resource for COPES, but gifts or transfers within five years of applying can cause a penalty period, and DSHS may seek repayment from the estate after death. Talk to an elder law attorney before transferring or selling.

### Can I sell my mother's house with power of attorney?

Possibly, if she signed a durable power of attorney that covers real estate while she had capacity. Without one, and if she can no longer sign, the family may need guardianship. Have the document reviewed before listing.

### Should I keep Mom's house empty or sell it?

It depends on the monthly cost of holding it, your family's finances, and how your parent is coping. A vacant house costs money every month and may affect insurance coverage, but some families keep it for a defined season as a bridge. Decide with numbers and a review date.
