# The Paperwork Is a Part-Time Job You Never Applied For

A man posted in a caregiver community recently about his mother. She is 82, she lives a few hours away, and she is mostly independent. She cooks, she goes to church, she does her own crossword. By any usual measure, she is doing fine.

And yet he said the paperwork has turned into a part-time job. An insurance claim that bounced. A Medicare letter nobody could explain. A prescription that lapsed before anyone noticed. A referral from her doctor that never got scheduled. He said he does not mind the work. What he minds is the feeling underneath it: that he is always a little behind, and that somewhere in the pile is the one thing he missed, the one that will matter. He tried a shared calendar with his sibling. It created more work, not less.

I am paraphrasing, because it is his story. But I have read versions of it a hundred times, and I lived my own. If your kitchen table has a stack of envelopes with your parent's name on them, or your email has a folder called "Mom stuff" that you are afraid to open, I want to say this first: you are not bad at organizing. You are doing a job that was never designed to be done by one tired person with no training and no pay, and the dread you feel is a reasonable response to that.

Let's talk about why the usual advice does not fix it, and what does.

## Why does managing a parent's paperwork feel so overwhelming?

It feels overwhelming because the paperwork itself is rarely the problem. The problem is that you have quietly become the one person who holds all the context, and every envelope is a question only you can answer.

Think about what a single Medicare Summary Notice actually asks of you. Is this a bill or not? Did Mom already get this care? Is that charge right? Who do I call? What did the doctor's office say last time? A stranger could read the page. Only you know the history. That is the part that wears you out. You are not just filing paper. You are acting as your parent's memory, her translator, and her quality control, all at once.

This matters because it changes what kind of solution you need. If the problem were disorganization, a binder would fix it. But most people who are drowning in a parent's paperwork already have a binder, or three. The binder is not the bottleneck. You are. When you are the only place the story lives, nothing can move unless you are available, awake, and in a good mood.

## Why don't binders, apps, and shared calendars fix it?

They fail because they store information, and information was never the scarce thing. Decisions are the scarce thing, and those still land on you.

The man in that thread tried a shared calendar with his sibling. It sounds smart. In practice, a shared calendar is a place to put reminders, and every reminder is another small demand on someone's attention. If nobody has agreed who owns which task, a shared calendar becomes a shared guilt board. Two people see the same red dot and each assumes the other will handle it.

Here is the premise most "get organized" advice quietly assumes: that if you only had the right system, the work would shrink. It does not. The work is the work. Insurance really is confusing. Prescriptions really do lapse. What a system can do is change who carries it, how often you have to think about it, and whether anyone else can pick it up if you get sick or go on vacation.

So the useful question is not "How do I get more organized?" It is "How do I stop being the single point of failure?" Once you ask that, the answers get a lot more practical.

## What is the first thing to do when your parent's paperwork is out of control?

Start by sorting every piece of paper into one of three piles, and notice how few of them are actually urgent. Almost all of the dread comes from not knowing which pile an envelope belongs in.

**Pile one: things with a deadline or a cost.** Bills, appeals, enrollment windows, a lapsing prescription, a form with a due date. These are real, and there are usually fewer than you fear.

**Pile two: things that need a decision but not a date.** A referral that should be scheduled, a plan comparison, a question for the doctor. Important, but they can wait for a calm Sunday.

**Pile three: information only.** Explanation of benefits statements, "this is not a bill" notices, confirmations. Most of what arrives falls here. You can read it, glance for anything strange, and let it go.

Once you see the piles, one afternoon of sorting usually turns "I am behind on everything" into "I have four things to do this week." That shift matters more than any app, because dread thrives on vagueness. It shrinks when it has edges.

## How do you make sure nothing important gets missed?

Create one page that holds the essentials, and keep a copy where someone other than you can find it. I call it a care brief. It does not need to be fancy. One sheet of paper or one document is enough.

Put these on it:

- Her full name, date of birth, and the name and phone number of each doctor and pharmacy.
- Her Medicare number and the name of her plan, or her Medicaid or private insurance information, stored somewhere safe and not emailed around casually.
- Every medication, the dose, and the pharmacy that fills it.
- Who has legal authority to help: a durable power of attorney for finances, a health care directive, and whether her doctors have a signed release allowing them to talk to you.
- Two or three people who can step in, and what each one is willing to do.

That last bullet is the one people skip, and it is the one that saves you. If you are the only person who knows where things are, you have built a house with one key. The brief is the spare key.

If your parent can still take part, do this with her, over tea, as something you are doing together. "Mom, I want to make sure that if I'm ever sick, someone can step in and nothing falls through" lands very differently than "I need your account numbers." I wrote about opening this kind of conversation in [what to do when a parent won't talk about care](/blog/parent-wont-talk-about-care-burien). You are not taking over. You are protecting her from the day you cannot do it all.

## Can you split the paperwork with a sibling without it becoming a fight?

Yes, if you split ownership instead of tasks. Give each person a whole lane that is theirs, start to finish, and stop asking each other for reminders.

Here is what goes wrong with the shared calendar approach. "Help me keep track of Mom's appointments" is a request for shared attention, and shared attention means nobody is in charge. Compare it with: "You own anything about her medications. Refills, pharmacy, doctor questions. I own insurance and billing. If it is in your lane, I will not check on it." That is a real division of labor. It also tends to be fairer, because it is visible.

Lanes that work well for many families are medications and medical appointments, insurance and billing, legal and financial documents, and the home itself (repairs, mail, safety). If you have a sibling who lives far away or says they "can't do the hands-on stuff," paperwork is often the best lane to hand them, because it can be done from anywhere. I have more on that dynamic in [when you're the only one: a letter to the sole family caregiver](/blog/sole-caregiver-siblings-burien).

Be honest about one thing: a lane only works if the person who owns it can say no and be believed. Agree up front that "I can't take this on" is an acceptable answer, and then reassign it rather than quietly absorbing it yourself.

## What paperwork should you handle before a crisis, not during one?

Handle three things now: the legal authority to act, a medical release, and a plain list of her accounts and bills. They are easy while she is well and extremely hard after a hospital stay or a diagnosis.

The first is a durable power of attorney, which lets a person you trust manage money and property if your parent cannot. The second is a health care directive and a signed release so her doctors are allowed to discuss her care with you. Without these, a bank or a clinic may politely refuse to talk to you at the exact moment you most need them to. In Washington, an elder law attorney can set these up and tell you what applies to your family. I am not a lawyer, and this is general information, not legal advice.

The third is boring and powerful: a list of every bill, where it is paid from, and when it is due. If her electricity or her supplemental insurance gets paid by autopay, write that down. If it does not, write that down too. Most scary surprises come from a bill nobody knew existed.

If you have already been through a sudden hospital discharge, you know why this matters. I wrote about that exact scramble in [the 4 PM Friday phone call](/blog/hospital-discharge-parent-burien).

## What about Medicare, and why does it feel like a trap this time of year?

It feels like a trap because it is a decision with a deadline, in the middle of the holidays, written in a language nobody taught you. Medicare's annual open enrollment runs from October 15 to December 7, which means that if your parent's plan or her medications have changed, the window to adjust her coverage for next year is open right now.

You do not have to figure it out alone, and you do not have to pay anyone to explain it. Washington runs a free, unbiased program called SHIBA (Statewide Health Insurance Benefits Advisors), run through the Office of the Insurance Commissioner, where trained volunteers will go through plan options with you or your parent. Their helpline is 800-562-6900. They do not sell insurance, which is the key difference between them and the many mailers and phone calls your parent has probably been receiving.

A few ways to make that appointment productive: bring her current medication list, her Medicare card, and her plan's annual notice of changes. Ask whether her pharmacy and doctors are still in network next year. And check what has changed in her prescriptions, because that, more than the premium, is usually what surprises families in January.

If your parent was recently in rehab and then lost coverage for it, that is a different, time-sensitive problem, and I walked through it in [the plateau letter](/blog/medicare-plateau-letter-rehab-burien).

## Where can Burien families get free help with this?

Several free local and state resources exist, and most families do not know about them.

**Community Living Connections (855-567-0252)** is Washington's front door for aging and long-term care questions. They can point you toward programs and caregiver supports in King County.

**SHIBA** (above) is the right call for Medicare questions.

**Your parent's doctor's office and pharmacy** often have a staff member who handles referrals and refills. Ask who that is by name, and write the name on the care brief. A single named contact is worth more than any app.

**The DSHS Adult Family Home Locator** is useful later, if care at home stops being workable, because it shows each licensed home's inspection history.

Burien has a number of licensed adult family homes, which means families here have real choices close to home. ## When does the paperwork mean it's time to think about more support?

When the paperwork is a symptom of a bigger gap, that is the moment to look at help beyond a better system. A missed prescription, a letter your mother cannot explain, or a bill she did not know she owed is sometimes just life. Sometimes it is the first quiet sign that she cannot safely manage this part of her life by herself.

Watch for a pattern, not a single event. Unopened mail piling up. The same bill paid twice, or not at all. Confusion about which pills she has taken. Phone calls from people she cannot quite describe. None of these is a verdict. Each is information, and the sooner you have it, the more options you have. I have written before about [how to tell when it's time](/blog/signs-its-time-residential-care-burien), and why so many families wait too long.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I manage my elderly parent's paperwork from far away?

Sort incoming mail into three piles (deadline or cost, needs a decision, information only), create a one-page care brief with medications, doctors, and legal documents, and ask a sibling or trusted friend to own one lane. Make sure you have a durable power of attorney and a medical release so you can talk to banks and doctors.

### Do I need power of attorney to handle my parent's bills and medical decisions?

Usually you need the right documents to act on someone's behalf: a durable power of attorney for finances and property, and a health care directive plus medical release for health matters. A Washington elder law attorney can advise on what fits your family.

### Is there free help with Medicare open enrollment in Washington?

Yes. SHIBA, run through the Washington Office of the Insurance Commissioner, offers free, unbiased Medicare counseling at 800-562-6900. Open enrollment runs October 15 to December 7.

### What if my siblings won't help with my parent's paperwork?

Give each sibling a whole lane, such as insurance and billing, rather than asking for general help. Paperwork can be done from anywhere, which makes it a good role for a far-away sibling.
