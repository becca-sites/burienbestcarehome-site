# When Dad Won't Let Anyone Help Care for Mom: What to Do When the Healthy Parent Is the Roadblock

Picture a phone call that goes something like this. Your mom is 90, she has dementia, and lately she is angry in a way she never used to be. She won't take the medication the doctor prescribed. Your dad is 85 and he is her only caregiver. He doesn't push her, he doesn't hide the pill in the applesauce, and when you bring up a home care aide he says, "She doesn't want a stranger in the house." Meanwhile he mentions, almost as an aside, that his ulcer is acting up again. You and your sister both work full time. You drive over when you can. You leave feeling sorry for him, frustrated with him, and guilty about both.

That isn't one family. It's a paraphrase of a post I read this week from an adult child on a caregiver forum, and it could have been written by a hundred different people in a hundred different kitchens. The replies underneath it were full of the same words: stuck, helpless, waiting. One person called it "a waiting game until an emergency happens." Another said families often have to let a parent fail before the parent will accept help.

I want to offer you something different from "wait for the crisis." Because there's a lot you can do right now, and most of it has nothing to do with winning an argument with your dad.

## Why does my dad refuse help caring for my mom?

Your dad refuses help because, to him, accepting it feels like admitting he has failed at the one job he promised to do. This is almost never stubbornness in the way we usually mean it. It's a vow.

Think about what your father actually said sixty years ago. He said "in sickness and in health," and he meant it. For a man of his generation, caring for his wife isn't a task he can delegate. It's who he is. When you suggest an aide, he doesn't hear "let's get you some support." He hears "you're not doing it right," or worse, "we're giving up on Mom."

There's research behind this. Penn State gerontologist Steven Zarit has found that spouses start using adult day programs later in an illness than adult children do, and when they do try them, they more often pull the person out after a short time. Spouses, in his words, "are likely to take on more than they can reasonably do." Add the habits of people who grew up in lean years, who saved every dollar for their children and can't imagine spending it on a stranger in the living room, and the picture gets clearer.

So the first truth to hold onto is this: your dad isn't refusing help because he doesn't love your mom. He's refusing because he loves her in the only way he knows how. That's worth saying out loud to yourself before you pick up the phone.

## Why is "just wait for a crisis" the wrong plan?

Waiting for a crisis is the wrong plan because the crisis doesn't land on your mom alone. It lands on both of your parents, and your dad is usually the one who breaks first.

Here's the premise most advice articles quietly assume: you have one patient. You don't. You have two. Your mom has dementia, and your dad is a caregiver in his mid-80s with his own body and his own risks. A well-known study from the University of Pittsburgh followed nearly 400 older spousal caregivers for four years and found that those under significant emotional strain had 63 percent higher mortality than non-caregivers. Later research linked high caregiving strain among spouses to a higher stroke risk, strongest among husbands caring for wives. (Those numbers come from studies reported by KFF Health News, and they're a few years old, but the pattern has held up in the years since.)

When people say "let him fail," what that often means in real life is a fall in the kitchen, a hospital admission for him, and your mom suddenly alone at home or placed overnight in a facility chosen in a panic. I wrote about how fast that goes in [the 4 PM Friday phone call from a hospital discharge planner](/blog/hospital-discharge-parent-burien). Families who've been through it will tell you it's the worst possible way to choose a place for someone you love.

So the real goal isn't to avoid the crisis by force. You probably can't. The goal is to make sure that when the crisis comes, it lands on a plan instead of on a blank page. That's a very different thing, and it's something you can start building today.

## What can I do right now when my dad won't accept help?

The most useful thing you can do right now is quietly build the safety net while you keep the relationship warm. Here are the moves I'd make, in roughly this order.

**1. Find out who holds the legal authority.** Before anything else, check whether someone other than your dad has durable power of attorney for your mom's health care and finances, and whether your dad has one naming someone for him. One caregiver in that forum thread said it plainly: confirm that someone other than the father can make decisions when needed. If the documents don't exist and your mom can still understand what she's signing, that window may be closing. In Washington, an elder law attorney can tell you what's possible. Please don't skip this one. Everything else gets harder without it.

**2. Write to the doctor.** Parents often take a physician's word more seriously than their kids' words. Send a short, factual letter or portal message to your mom's doctor (and your dad's) describing what you're seeing: the refused medication, the escalating anger, your dad's ulcer, how tired he looks. Ask the doctor to raise caregiver support at the next appointment. Many families find that "the doctor said I should have some help" lands in a way that "your kids think you need help" never does.

**3. Change the question.** Instead of "Dad, we need to get Mom an aide," try questions that start from his values. "What would happen to Mom if you ended up in the hospital for a week?" "What would you want me to do?" "If I could take one thing off your plate so you could keep doing the parts only you can do, what would it be?" You are not trying to replace him. You are trying to protect his role by giving it a backup. If you want more on the conversation itself, [when a parent won't talk about care](/blog/parent-wont-talk-about-care-burien) has scripts that have helped families I know.

**4. Offer help that doesn't look like help.** A neighbor who "just drops by" on Tuesdays. A grocery delivery. A house cleaner "because I'm worried about your allergies." A standing weekly lunch where you take Mom for a drive and he gets two hours. A lot of fathers will accept a favor from a friend long before they'll accept a paid caregiver, and each small yes teaches your parents that a person coming through the door doesn't mean the end of the world.

**5. Bring in a neutral professional.** One caregiver in the thread described having an elder care specialist evaluate her parents, presented to her dad as a way to make his life easier. It eventually led to full-time care. In King County, Community Living Connections (855-567-0252) is a free starting point for help at home, adult day programs, and caregiver support, and the Alzheimer's Association has a 24-hour helpline at 800-272-3900. A voice from outside the family can say what you can't.

**6. Tour a home before you need one.** This is the one most families skip, and it matters the most. Visit two or three adult family homes now, on a calm Tuesday, before anything is urgent. Ask about openings, about what the waitlist looks like, and about respite stays. Write down what you learn. If you have never toured one, I put together [what to look for when touring an adult family home](/blog/touring-adult-family-home-burien) so you know what's worth asking.

None of these steps requires your father to say yes. That's the whole point. They are all things you can do while he is still saying no.

## What is respite care, and why is it the gentlest first step?

Respite care is a short stay, usually a few days to a few weeks, in a care setting that gives the family caregiver a real break. It is often the easiest "yes" to get from a reluctant spouse, because it has an end date.

Think about how different these two sentences sound to your dad. "We need to move Mom into a home" is a verdict. "You've got a doctor's appointment and you're exhausted, so Mom is going to stay somewhere nice for five days while you rest" is a favor, and it's temporary. He gets to keep his vow. He just gets to keep it with a night of sleep behind him.

A respite stay does something else that's easy to miss. It gives everyone information. Your dad sees that your mom is okay without him in the room. Your mom has a chance to settle into a smaller, calmer environment, which, for many people with dementia, softens the late-afternoon agitation. And you, the adult child, get to see what a good small home actually looks like when it's running. In a small home like ours here in Burien, there are only a handful of residents for each caregiver, and there are no long corridors or shift changes of strangers. For a woman who is angry because the world has become confusing, fewer faces and fewer transitions can be the difference between a good day and a hard one.

That's also why families often tell me the first respite stay was the turning point. Not because it "convinced" their dad, but because it showed him a version of care that didn't take his wife away from him. ## What if my mom refuses her medication and my dad won't push?

If your mom is refusing medication and your dad won't push, bring it to her doctor right away, because refusal often means something specific that can be solved. It isn't a character flaw.

People with dementia refuse pills for lots of reasons: the pill is hard to swallow, the taste is bad, they don't recognize it, they're paranoid about being poisoned, or they simply don't remember why they're taking it. A clinician can often change the form, the timing, or the drug itself. Please don't start hiding medication in food on your own. Washington families should ask the prescriber or a pharmacist what's safe before changing how a medication is given, because some pills can't be crushed and some mixing methods change how the drug works. And the pattern in her anger (is it worst at 4 or 5 in the afternoon?) is exactly what a doctor needs to hear. I covered that pattern in [the 5 o'clock shadow of sundowning](/blog/sundowning-breaking-point-dementia-burien).

Your dad's reluctance to force her isn't weakness. He's protecting the last shred of her dignity and her choice. You can honor that and still say, "Dad, I'd like us to ask the doctor to find a way that doesn't need a fight."

## What does Washington offer that families don't know about?

Washington has a few supports that matter more than most families realize, and some are brand new this year.

**WA Cares Fund.** The state's long-term care benefit opened for access in July 2026, with a lifetime maximum of $36,500 in 2026 dollars that increases with inflation. It can pay for in-home personal care, adult day services, respite for family caregivers, dementia and behavioral supports, and care in settings including adult family homes. A family member who regularly helps may even qualify to become a paid caregiver. Eligibility depends on contribution and care-need requirements, so check the "how to qualify" page at [wacaresfund.wa.gov](https://wacaresfund.wa.gov) before you assume anything either way. One more thing worth knowing: the benefit is modest, so it works best as a bridge, like a respite stay or a few months of in-home help, rather than a full solution.

**Medicaid and COPES.** If money is the quiet reason your dad says no ("we can't afford it"), know that Washington's Medicaid program can help pay for adult family home care for people who qualify, and many adult family homes, including ours, work with families using Medicaid COPES. I walked through the process step by step in [the COPES and Medicaid guide](/blog/copes-medicaid-adult-family-home-burien). If your parents own their house and that's part of the equation, my real estate business, [Your Next Step Team](https://yournextstepteam.com), helps Washington families think through selling a family home when the time comes.

**Adult Protective Services.** I want to say this one carefully, because no one wants to be the child who "called on Dad." But if your mom is being left in danger, if her basic needs aren't being met, or if your dad's own self-neglect has become serious, Washington's APS accepts reports at 1-877-734-6277 for concerns about abuse, neglect, self-neglect, and exploitation of vulnerable adults. Caregiver burnout can tip into neglect even in the most loving marriages, and one of the commenters in that forum thread warned about exactly that. Calling isn't betrayal. Sometimes it's the only door that opens.

## How do I stop feeling so guilty?

You stop feeling guilty by recognizing that guilt is the wrong name for what you're feeling. The top reply in that caregiver thread said it better than I can: guilt implies responsibility you don't actually have, and what you're feeling is closer to grief over helplessness.

You can't make an 85-year-old man change. You can't cure your mother's dementia. You can set conditions, build the safety net, tell the truth, show up, and keep the door open. That's not nothing. It's a lot. And it is the work of someone who loves both of their parents at once, which is its own kind of heavy.

If your mother is already slipping away in ways that are hard to name, you might also find comfort in [what ambiguous loss does to families](/blog/ambiguous-loss-dementia-parent-burien). You're allowed to grieve someone who is still in the room. And if you're one of two siblings carrying this and one of you is doing most of it, [this piece on the sole caregiver among siblings](/blog/sole-caregiver-siblings-burien) is for you.

## What will it look like when he finally says yes?

When he finally says yes, it usually doesn't sound like a concession. It sounds like a small, tired sentence: "Maybe we should try it for a week."

That moment almost always comes after something small and scary: a fall, a night of wandering, a day when Mom didn't recognize him, a hospital visit that wasn't supposed to be serious. The families who do best are the ones who were ready for that sentence. They had the paperwork. They had toured a place. They knew who to call. When Dad said "maybe," they could say, "I already found somewhere. Want to see it?" and not "Okay, let me start looking."

You can be that family. You don't have to be pushy about it or wear anyone down. You only have to prepare, quietly, so that when the door opens even a crack, you've got somewhere to walk them through.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I force my father to accept help for my mother?

No, as long as your father is competent, he can decline services, and you can't override that. What you can do is confirm who holds power of attorney, bring concerns to the doctors, and build a plan for when he's ready. If either parent is in danger, you can contact Washington Adult Protective Services at 1-877-734-6277.

### How do I convince my elderly father to try respite care?

Present it as a short, temporary break for him that ends on a specific date, ideally tied to a medical appointment or his own health. Starting with a three to five day stay in a small home lowers the stakes and lets him see his wife is cared for.

### What are the signs my father is too exhausted to keep caregiving?

Look for weight loss, missed doctor appointments, falls or near-falls, irritability, confusion about his own medications, and a house that is falling behind. Any of these means it's time to bring in more support.

### Does WA Cares pay for an adult family home in Burien?

WA Cares lists adult family homes among covered settings, but benefits are capped at a lifetime maximum of $36,500 in 2026 and eligibility rules apply. Check [wacaresfund.wa.gov](https://wacaresfund.wa.gov), and ask the home whether it works with WA Cares and Medicaid.

### Does Medicaid cover adult family homes in Washington State?

Yes, Washington Medicaid can help pay for adult family home care for people who meet the financial and care-need requirements, usually through the COPES program. Many Burien-area homes accept Medicaid, and a DSHS case manager can help start the assessment.

### How do I compare adult family homes in Burien?

Start with the DSHS Adult Family Home Locator, which lists every licensed home in Burien along with its inspection history, so you can compare them before you visit. Then tour your top two or three in person.
