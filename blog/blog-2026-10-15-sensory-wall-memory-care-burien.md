# Why Our Hallway Has 24 Handmade Sensory Stations (And What They Actually Do for Residents with Dementia)

There is a moment in the middle of the afternoon that every caregiver knows. It happens around 2:30 or 3:00, when the light shifts and something in the house gets restless. A resident who was napping on the couch ten minutes ago is up and moving. She is not going anywhere in particular. She is just going. Down the hall, back to the living room, into the kitchen, back down the hall. Her hands are busy but empty. She is pulling at the hem of her sweater. She is opening a drawer and closing it. She is looking for something she cannot name.

If you have cared for a parent with dementia, you know this moment. You have lived it at 11pm in your own house, watching your mother circle the kitchen for the third time in twenty minutes, picking up a dish towel and putting it down, opening the refrigerator and staring at the shelves. You asked her what she needed. She said she was fine. You both knew that was not quite the answer.

In the caregiving communities I follow, families describe this restlessness as one of the hardest things to witness. Not because it is dangerous. Because it feels like watching someone search for a self they cannot locate. One daughter on a caregiver forum described it as her mother not being upset so much as having hands that no longer knew what to do, while some part of her still remembered that they used to.

That idea changed how I thought about our hallway.

## What Is a Sensory Wall in a Memory Care Home?

A sensory wall is a collection of mounted stations along a hallway or common area that give residents something meaningful to touch, see, hear, or interact with as they move through the space. Each station is designed to engage a different sense or motor skill: tactile textures, visual puzzles, fine motor activities, creative tools, and familiar objects that connect to long-term memory.

At Burien Best Care Home, our hallway features 24 handmade sensory stations. Not purchased from a catalog. Not installed by a contractor. Built by hand, mounted in wooden frames, and placed at the right height for someone walking or standing or seated in a wheelchair.

They include things like a wooden activity board with an abacus, clock, gears, and lacing holes. An I-spy shadowbox filled with small everyday objects. A Magna Doodle for drawing and erasing. A rainbow xylophone with color-coded sheet music cards for "Mary Had a Little Lamb" and "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star." Rope spiral art for tracing with a fingertip. Convex mirrors that shift and shimmer. Sand art you can turn upside down and watch resettle. Fidget spinners mounted on a wooden airplane. Pinwheels that spin with a breath. Soft chenille textile pieces. Splash paintings in blues and greens.

Every station does something different. Together, they turn a hallway into a place worth being.

## Why Does Sensory Engagement Matter for People with Dementia?

Here is what I have learned from watching residents walk that hallway: dementia takes a lot of things. It takes names. It takes the sequence of getting dressed. It takes the thread of a conversation halfway through. But it does not take the hands. Not for a long time. The hands remember how to turn a knob, how to trace a shape, how to tap a rhythm on a xylophone. The hands remember music before the mouth remembers lyrics.

This is not just something I have observed. Research supports it. The brain continues to process sensory input even as cognitive function declines. Touch, color, sound, and texture can reach a person when words cannot. Occupational therapists have long used homelike, multisensory environments to ease the restlessness and distress that often come with dementia, and sensory approaches are now a common part of dementia care, even as researchers keep studying which ones work best for which people.

But here is what the research papers do not capture, and what we see every day: the look on a resident's face when she stops mid-walk, reaches out, and runs her fingers across the rope spiral art on the wall. She is not "receiving sensory stimulation." She is doing something that feels good. She is touching something interesting. She is having a moment where her body and her attention line up, and she is present. That matters more than any clinical term for it.

## How Do Sensory Stations Help with Wandering and Agitation?

If you have toured memory care facilities, you have probably heard the word "redirection." It means gently guiding a resident away from a behavior that could be harmful (trying to leave the house, pulling at medical equipment, becoming increasingly agitated) and toward something calming or engaging.

In a large facility, redirection usually means a staff member physically intervening. Walking over, making eye contact, offering a snack or a seat. That works, but it requires someone to be available every time, and in a large building where one staff member may be covering many residents, that is not always instant.

Our sensory wall does something different. It redirects without anyone having to be there in that exact second.

A resident walking the hallway with restless hands passes the activity board. She stops. She turns the gears. She threads the lacing. Three minutes go by. The agitation in her shoulders has softened. She moves on to the next station, the Magna Doodle, and draws a circle. Then another circle. Then she smiles at it and keeps walking, but slower now. Calmer.

This is not a replacement for attentive care. Our team is small and so is our house, so someone is always close. But the wall gives residents something our staff cannot give them alone: independent agency. The ability to choose to stop, choose to engage, choose to move on. In a disease that strips away choices hour by hour, a hallway full of choices is not a small thing.

## What Makes Handmade Stations Different from Commercial Products?

You can buy sensory boards from medical supply catalogs. They exist. They are typically plastic, uniform, and designed to look clinical. They serve the same basic function. So why build them by hand?

Honestly, because a hallway full of plastic medical equipment does not feel like a home. It feels like a ward.

Our stations are mounted in dark wooden frames that match the house. The textures are real: actual rope, actual chenille, actual sand. The xylophone is a real instrument with real wooden mallets, not a plastic toy. The Magna Doodle is a real Magna Doodle, because every resident in this house grew up in a world where that was a real thing you played with at your grandmother's kitchen table.

Familiarity matters in dementia care more than almost anything else. When someone with Alzheimer's picks up a wooden mallet and taps a xylophone, she is not doing an "activity." She is doing something her hands have done before. The music cards on the wall say "Mary Had a Little Lamb" in color-coded notes, and some part of her knows that song without needing to read the words. Her fingers find the notes. The melody comes out. And for thirty seconds, she is playing music, the way she used to play music, in a hallway that feels like a house, because it is a house.

That is the difference. Not the materials. The feeling.

## Do Sensory Walls Actually Reduce Anxiety in Residents?

I cannot cite a randomized controlled trial about our specific hallway. But I can tell you what I have seen since we installed these stations.

The afternoons are calmer. The late-day restlessness that families and clinicians call "sundowning" still happens, because it is a feature of the disease, not something a wall can cure. But the peak of it is lower. Residents who might otherwise pace the full length of the hall again and again tend to stop along the way. They engage with a station for a minute or two, then continue. The pacing becomes a walk. The walk becomes a series of small, chosen interactions. The difference is subtle from the outside and enormous from the inside.

Residents who might otherwise need to be redirected again and again often redirect themselves at the stations. Some residents are drawn to the I-spy shadowbox and quietly name the objects they find. Others gravitate toward the blue textile art and run a palm across the chenille over and over, the way you might pet a cat. You can see it soothe them, right down to their breathing.

Families notice it too. Visits in the afternoon can feel more like visits again. A parent who can no longer tell you what day it is may still stand at the xylophone and tap out a rhythm, and for a lot of families, that moment is enough.

## What Should Families Look for When Touring a Memory Care Home?

If you are touring adult family homes or memory care facilities in Burien, King County, or anywhere in Washington State, here are some things worth noticing about the environment:

**Look at the walls.** Are they bare and institutional, or do they have things a person can touch and interact with? A bare hallway tells you something. A hallway with engagement tells you something else.

**Ask about redirection tools.** When a resident becomes restless or agitated, what does the staff do? If the answer is only "we redirect them," ask what they redirect them to. A sensory wall, a garden, a music player, a familiar task: these are concrete answers. "We handle it" is not.

**Watch the residents, not the staff.** During your tour, look at what residents are doing. Are they sitting in chairs facing a television? Are they in their rooms? Or are they moving through the house, stopping at things, picking things up, engaging with their surroundings? The second picture is the one you want.

**Ask about personalization.** Not every resident responds to the same sensory input. Some people are drawn to visual stimulation (mirrors, colors, moving objects). Some prefer tactile (textures, fabrics, objects to manipulate). Some respond to auditory (music, chimes, familiar sounds). A good memory care home knows which residents gravitate toward which stations and can tell you about individual preferences.

**Trust your gut.** When you walk through the front door, does it feel like a home or a facility? Does the hallway feel like a place someone would want to walk through? Does the space feel lived-in, warm, and purposeful? These are not soft questions. They are the questions that matter most.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a sensory wall in a care home?

A sensory wall is a collection of mounted stations along a hallway or common area designed to engage residents through touch, sight, sound, and fine motor interaction. At Burien Best Care Home, our sensory wall features 24 handmade stations including activity boards, tactile art, visual puzzles, musical instruments, and creative tools.

### Do sensory activities help with dementia?

Yes. Research shows that the brain continues to process sensory input even as cognitive function declines. Sensory activities can reduce anxiety and agitation, provide meaningful engagement, and support independent interaction for residents with Alzheimer's and other forms of dementia.

### What is sundowning, and can a sensory wall help?

Sundowning is increased confusion, agitation, or restlessness that many people with dementia experience in the late afternoon and evening. While a sensory wall cannot prevent sundowning, it can reduce its intensity by giving residents meaningful points of engagement during the hours when restlessness typically peaks.

### How is an adult family home different from a large memory care facility?

An adult family home in Washington State is a licensed private residence caring for up to eight residents, so the caregivers know each person well. Large facilities may care for dozens or even a hundred or more residents. The smaller setting allows for more personalized, homelike care.
