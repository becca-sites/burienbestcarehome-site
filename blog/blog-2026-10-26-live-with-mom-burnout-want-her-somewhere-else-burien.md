# I Live With My Mom and I'm Running on Empty: Is It Wrong to Want Her Somewhere Else?

Here is a post I keep thinking about. A woman has been living with her mother for six years. She cared for her father there until he died. Now her mother is on hospice at home for heart failure, mostly in bed, low in spirits, and refusing visitors, outings, and anything that looks like adult day care. Her sister lives a mile away and helps a few hours at a time. The hospice only offers five-day respite stays. She writes that her mental health is hanging by a thread, that she is stress-eating, that she cries more than she used to. And then she asks the question that takes so much nerve to type: would it be wrong to ask whether her mother could stay at a private care home for a month, just so she can breathe?

I am paraphrasing, because it is her story and not mine. But I want you to hear what happened in the replies, because it is the part that matters. They split right down the middle. Some people were kind and practical. Others were hard. She lives rent-free, they said, so caregiving is the deal. It is her mother's house, so she can't ask her mother to leave. One person suggested a month away was unrealistic.

If you are reading this at 11pm because you live with your mom or dad and some version of that question is sitting on your chest, I want to say the first thing out loud: you are not a bad daughter or son for asking it. You asked it because you are at the end of something. Let's look at what that something actually is, and what you can do about it.

## Is it wrong to want my parent to live somewhere else?

No, it is not wrong to want your parent to have care somewhere else, because needing more care than one tired person can provide is a fact about the care, not a flaw in your love. Wanting a different arrangement is what a person does when the current one has stopped working.

It helps to separate three things that get tangled together in your head at night. The first is a feeling: "I can't do this anymore." The second is a fact: one person, even a devoted one, cannot safely provide round-the-clock supervision for months or years. The third is a story: "If I really loved her, I would find a way." The feeling is information. The fact is just arithmetic. It is only the story that makes you a villain, and the story is the one piece of this that is not true.

Caregiving is also not one job. When a parent needs help with groceries and medications, a loving adult child can do it. When the same parent needs turning, lifting, toileting, nighttime checks, and someone awake and alert in case something happens, the job has changed into something that professional care teams staff with shifts. They rotate because the work wears people down. Nobody asked you to cover three shifts a day by yourself, and when you try, the failure is in the design, not in you.

## Why does "you live rent-free, so you owe her" feel true?

It feels true because it sounds like a fair trade, but it is a trade nobody ever agreed to, and the terms keep changing without anyone being asked.

Here is a premise that most advice about this situation quietly assumes: that living in a parent's home creates a debt that care repays. Look at it closely and it falls apart. When you moved in, what exactly did you promise? Probably company. Probably help with the garden, the bills, the driving. Probably the understanding that someone would be around if Dad or Mom needed a hand. That was the arrangement in your head, and it may have been a good one, a real gift to both of you.

What you did not agree to, because no one could have, was a future in which your mother needs hospice-level help at home and the housing becomes the "payment" for twenty-four hour nursing. Rent is a number. Caregiving at this level has no ceiling. A deal with no ceiling and no end date is not a fair trade. It is a trap with a nice address.

There is a practical side too. If you have been providing real care with no written agreement, nobody has been tracking what it is worth, and that matters later. In Washington, Medicaid looks back sixty months at transfers of money and assets, and payments to family for past care can be read as gifts. If a care agreement ever makes sense for your family, it needs to be written before the care happens, at a fair rate, ideally with an elder law attorney's help. I cover how that fits into the larger funding picture in [the COPES and Medicaid walkthrough](/blog/copes-medicaid-adult-family-home-burien). For now the point is a simpler one: the "you owe her" accounting was never done honestly. Anyone who says you owe her is doing math with numbers nobody wrote down.

## Is this caregiver burnout, or am I just tired?

It is probably burnout if rest no longer helps, because ordinary tiredness lifts after a good night's sleep and burnout does not.

You know the difference by now. Tiredness sounds like "I need a weekend." Burnout sounds like "I dread the sound of her calling my name, and then I hate myself for dreading it." Here are signs I would take seriously, and none of them is a character defect:

- You are sleeping badly even when you do get the chance to sleep.
- You are eating, drinking, or scrolling more than you want to, just to get through the day.
- You cry in the car, the shower, or the grocery aisle, and the crying surprises you.
- You feel resentment toward the person you love, and then guilt about the resentment.
- You have stopped seeing friends, your own doctor, or the things that used to be yours.
- You catch yourself being short with her, or fantasizing about it all being over, and it scares you.

If you recognized yourself, please hear this: a month would not fix it. Several people in that thread pointed out that burnout takes far longer than a month to recover from, and they are right. A month of relief is real and worth fighting for, but it is a first aid kit, not a cure. Recovery comes from changing the structure so the load is not entirely yours again afterward.

It is also worth saying that this is common. In Washington alone there are more than a million unpaid family caregivers, and nationally the number is around 43 million. Most of them are women, and the average caregiver is a woman in her late forties caring for a widowed mother. You are in a very large, very tired company.

## What are my real options when my parent refuses help?

Your options form a ladder, and you can climb one rung at a time, starting with the ones that do not require your mother to agree to anything.

**1. Make a specific ask of the sibling or relative.** "Can you help more?" is easy to deflect. "Can you take Tuesday and Thursday nights, starting next week?" is not. Put it in a text so there is a record. If a family meeting would help, hold it with a short agenda: what Mom needs, what you can no longer do, what each person will take on. I wrote more about the sibling dynamic in [the sole caregiver and siblings post](/blog/sole-caregiver-siblings-burien).

**2. Use the professionals already in the room.** If your parent is on hospice, the hospice social worker and chaplain are part of the team, and part of their job is supporting you. Ask for a caregiver conversation and say the true sentence: "I am not able to keep doing this alone." Ask what aide hours they can add and what respite options exist.

**3. Tap the free local help.** Community Living Connections (855-567-0252) is a good first call in King County for in-home services, adult day programs, and caregiver support. Washington's Family Caregiver Support Program, run through your local Area Agency on Aging, offers caregiver counseling, training, and in some cases funds for respite. If you work, ask your employer about Washington Paid Family and Medical Leave, which can provide up to twelve weeks of partial pay to care for a family member with a serious health condition.

**4. Hire paid help in set shifts.** Even a few hours of regular in-home help, on the same days each week, changes how the week feels. Your mother may resist a stranger. Start with something small and routine, like a weekly bath or a Tuesday lunch visit, so the stranger becomes familiar.

**5. Try a respite stay.** This is the one most people don't know how to arrange, so I'll give it its own section next.

**6. Consider a different living arrangement, for either of you.** Sometimes the answer is that your parent moves to a home with 24-hour staffing. Sometimes the answer is that you move out and care comes to her. Both are legitimate. Please don't let anyone tell you the only two choices are "stay forever" and "abandon her." There is a wide middle, and the middle is where most families end up.

## What is respite care, and how does it work at a Burien adult family home?

Respite care is a short stay, from a few days to a few weeks, in a care setting that gives the family caregiver a real break while the parent is looked after around the clock. It is usually the gentlest first step toward a longer-term plan, because it has an end date.

Here is how it tends to work at a small adult family home in the Burien area. Your parent gets a room of her own, three meals a day, help with bathing, toileting, medications, and mobility, and caregivers who stay awake and present. Because there are only a few residents, the caregiver learns your mother's routines quickly: how she likes her tea, which hour she gets restless, what makes her laugh. If she is on hospice, the hospice team can keep visiting her where she is staying. Hospice follows the patient, not the address.

The thing the woman in that thread ran into is worth knowing about. Medicare's hospice benefit covers short inpatient respite stays, but only up to five days in a row. That is real help for a weekend, and not nearly enough for someone who has been running on empty for months. Longer respite in a private adult family home is generally paid privately, though some families use veterans' benefits, long-term care insurance, or Washington programs. Rates vary a lot from home to home, so ask directly and ask for it in writing.

If you decide to look, here is what to ask:

- Can you take a respite stay for the dates I need, and is there a minimum length?
- Are you comfortable with a person who is on hospice, or who needs two-person help for transfers?
- Who is awake overnight, and how many residents does that person look after?
- How do you keep me informed, and can I visit any time?
- What happens if a respite stay turns out to be the right fit and we want to extend it?

## What if a month of rest shows me she needs to live somewhere else?

Then you will have learned something true, and you will have learned it from a position of rest instead of panic.

This is the part nobody says out loud: a respite stay is also a trial. Many families discover that their parent, who swore she would never accept help, eats better, laughs more, and sleeps through the night with a team around her. Others discover the opposite, that she is unhappy and the move isn't right, and they bring her home with a plan for more support. Either answer is useful. What you can't get from a crisis is the chance to choose.

If it does become permanent, a few practical threads are worth pulling now. If she owns the house you share, a longer-term move raises questions about what happens to it, whether it is kept for you, rented, or sold. If selling comes up, my real estate practice, [Your Next Step Team](https://yournextstepteam.com), helps Washington families through exactly that kind of transition. And if money is the worry, [paying for senior care](/blog/paying-for-senior-care-burien) and the Medicaid COPES program can change the math more than most families expect. The first weeks after a move are also hard in their own way, and [what to expect in the first weeks after placement](/blog/first-weeks-after-placement-burien) can help you prepare for the guilt that shows up on day three.

Moving a parent is not the same as evicting her. Say that to yourself until it feels true. Evicting someone means putting them out. What you are doing is making sure the person you love is looked after by people who can do it safely, while you go back to being her daughter or son and not her night nurse.

## How do I talk to my parent and my family about this?

Start with what you are asking for, not with what you are giving up on. Parents hear "I can't do this" as rejection and "I want to keep you safe and keep us close" as love.

With your mother, try something like: "Mom, I want to be your daughter, not just your caregiver. I am so tired that I am not the daughter I want to be. I would like to try a short stay somewhere with a team around you, just two weeks, and then we decide together what comes next." Notice the structure: it names the love, it is honest about the tiredness, it has an end date, and it leaves the next decision open. If conversations like this are difficult in your family, [when a parent won't talk about care](/blog/parent-wont-talk-about-care-burien) has more scripts.

With your sibling, be specific and kind, and don't wait to be asked. "I have reached my limit. Here's what I need by the first of next month. If we can't cover it ourselves, I'd like us to look at a respite stay together, and I'd like you to help me tour two places." Siblings who are far away are usually less cold than they seem. They are often scared, and scared people stay quiet.

With the critics, the people in the comments, the cousin who says it's her house, the neighbor who says family takes care of family: you are allowed to say nothing. You are allowed to say, "Thank you, we're working out what's best for Mom and for me." No one else is in your house at 3am.

## What would I tell the woman in that post?

I would tell her that her question was not a confession. It was a flare sent up from someone who has been carrying a weight alone for six years, and the right response to a flare is to send help, not to hand her a bill.

I would tell her that the five-day limit on hospice respite is a policy, not a verdict on how much rest she is allowed to need. I would tell her to call the hospice social worker this week, to call Community Living Connections, and to ask her sister for two specific nights. I would tell her that a respite stay somewhere gentle is not giving up on her mother, and that her mother, deep down, may be just as worried about wearing her out as she is about being worn out.

And I would tell her this: the version of you who keeps going until you collapse is not the version your mother wants. She wants you. She wants the daughter who can still laugh, who can sit on the edge of the bed and hold her hand without counting the hours. Giving that daughter back to her is not abandonment. It is the whole point.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I put my mother in respite care without her agreeing?

If your mother is competent, she has the right to decline, and you can't override that. If she lacks capacity, the person with durable power of attorney for health care can make decisions on her behalf, ideally with her doctor's input. Either way, a short, time-limited stay framed around her safety and your health is more likely to get a yes than an ultimatum.

### How long can a parent on hospice stay in respite care?

Under Medicare's hospice benefit, inpatient respite care is limited to five consecutive days at a time. Longer stays in a private adult family home are usually paid privately or through other benefits, and the hospice team can keep visiting your parent wherever she stays.

### Is it okay to ask my parent to move out of their own house?

It is okay to ask for a different arrangement when the current one is unsafe or unsustainable, but there are usually more options than "she moves out." Some families bring in paid help, some have the adult child move out, and some choose a care home. A family meeting and an elder law attorney can help you sort out the best fit.

### Does an adult child who lives with a parent owe unlimited care?

No. Living in a parent's home does not create an unlimited caregiving obligation. Arrangements like this work best when expectations are written down, reviewed as needs change, and backed by outside help when the care becomes more than one person can safely provide.

### Does Medicaid cover adult family homes in Washington State?

Yes, Washington Medicaid can help pay for adult family home care for people who meet the financial and care-need requirements, usually through the COPES program. Many Burien-area homes accept Medicaid, and a DSHS case manager can start the assessment.
